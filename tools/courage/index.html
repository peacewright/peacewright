Courage Is a Tool We Can Practice

When something is wrong and responding carries a cost, Courage can help us move from concern to a thoughtful next step.

Sometimes the need for Courage is obvious. Other times it appears in quieter moments, when something feels wrong but speaking or acting would be uncomfortable, uncertain, or risky. Sometimes the difficult moment is our own: we realize we have caused harm, stayed silent when we wish we had spoken, or need to admit that we were wrong.

These situations can be very different from one another, but they may share the same question: What do we do when we recognize that something is wrong, but doing something about it feels difficult? That is where Courage becomes a tool.

Courage is more than a bold move

We often picture courage as something dramatic: confronting danger, making a public stand, or stepping into a difficult situation. Those moments can require tremendous courage, but they are not the only places courage appears. Often it is quieter and less visible.

Courage can mean speaking up when it would be easier to stay silent, checking on someone who may need help, asking others to step in, or refusing to go along with something we know is wrong. It can also mean turning that same honesty toward ourselves—listening when someone tells us we caused harm, resisting the urge to become defensive, and taking responsibility for our part in what happened.

Courage does not require perfect words, complete certainty, or a dramatic gesture. Sometimes it begins with something much simpler: noticing what is happening, thinking carefully about what the moment calls for, and deciding not to look away.

Courage is not the same as confrontation

Standing against harm does not always mean confronting the person causing it. Sometimes direct intervention is appropriate, but sometimes another response is safer or more useful. Someone else may have more authority, training, influence, or ability to help, and a quiet or private response may accomplish more than a public challenge.

There is also an important difference between harm and disagreement. People can hold opposing political, religious, cultural, or personal beliefs without either person being under attack. Courage should not become an excuse to treat every disagreement as an emergency.

The harder work is developing the judgment to recognize when a response may be needed and then deciding what constructive action is available to us. That action may be direct, but it can also be indirect, private, collective, delayed, or focused on getting help. The courageous question is not always, “How do I confront this?” Sometimes the better question is, “What can I do that helps?”

Courage works with other tools

Courage rarely works alone. It may require Empathy to understand what someone else is experiencing, Listening before assuming we know what they need, or Self-control so our own fear or anger does not make the situation worse. It may also require Truthfulness, Protection, Humility, or other tools depending on the circumstances.

The Step UP! framework for bystander intervention approaches helping in a similar way. It connects awareness, perspective-taking, values, courage, and practical strategies rather than assuming that every situation has one correct response. That is an important distinction. Courage is not simply the willingness to act; it is the willingness to act thoughtfully.

The purpose of Courage is not to make us reckless. It is to make fear, discomfort, embarrassment, or social pressure one consideration in our decision—not the thing that automatically makes the decision for us.

Small courage matters

It is easy to recognize courage when something extraordinary happens, but much of the work occurs in ordinary moments. Speaking when silence would be easier, offering support, refusing to participate in harm, admitting we were wrong, or getting help instead of assuming someone else will act may not look dramatic from the outside. They can still matter greatly to the person affected.

Peacemaking is not only found in grand gestures. The craft is also developed in ordinary situations, where we learn to choose our response instead of allowing fear, habit, discomfort, or the crowd to choose it for us.

That matters because courage, like any other tool, becomes more familiar through use. Small moments can help us learn how we respond under pressure, what tends to hold us back, and where our judgment still needs work.

Courage can be practiced before we need it

One of the hardest parts of a difficult situation is that it often arrives without warning. We are trying to understand what is happening while also deciding what to do, which is not an ideal time to discover our options for the first time.

That raises an important question: can Courage be practiced?

Research into bystander intervention gives us reason to explore that idea. In a 2023 article in the Journal of Character and Leadership Development, Everett Spain, Yasmine Konheim-Kalkstein, Ryan Erbe, Corrine Wilsey, and Stacey Rosenberg described scenario-based role-playing workshops at West Point. Cadets practiced different roles, including upstander, witness, and person causing harm. The authors reported greater participant engagement than with traditional bystander training and described improvisational role-play as a promising approach, while also noting that more research is needed.

A 2020 exploratory study by Davide Dal Cason, Annalisa Casini, and Catherine Hellemans examined moral courage and bystander intervention in workplace bullying scenarios. The researchers found preliminary evidence of a relationship between moral courage and intervention while emphasizing that the relationship is complex and requires further study.

None of this means we can rehearse every situation we might encounter. We cannot. But we can think through difficult moments before we are standing inside one. We can ask what would tell us that something is wrong, what we could safely do, who else could help, what might make the situation worse, and what might stop us from acting.

That last question may be the most useful. Fear, uncertainty, embarrassment, loyalty, social pressure, or the assumption that someone else will act can all shape our response. Recognizing those pressures beforehand may make them easier to recognize when the moment arrives.

Specialized situations deserve specialized knowledge

Courage does not make someone an expert. Different forms of harm involve different risks, and the right response in one situation may be completely wrong in another. Organizations that specialize in particular problems have developed training and intervention strategies based on years of experience and research.

For example, RAINN's CARE approach to sexual-violence intervention offers several options, including creating a distraction, asking directly whether someone needs help, rallying other people, and extending support afterward. Those strategies are useful in that particular context, just as other situations have their own experts, resources, and practices.

Part of developing Courage is knowing when our best action is finding someone who knows more than we do. A Peacewright does not need to become an expert in everything. We can learn from the people who are already doing the work.

Courage does not guarantee an outcome

There is another uncomfortable truth about Courage: doing something does not guarantee that things will turn out well. We can misunderstand a situation, choose the wrong approach, have our help rejected, freeze when we expected ourselves to act, or intervene and still fail to prevent harm.

Courage cannot remove uncertainty. What it can do is help us act thoughtfully despite that uncertainty.

There are also legitimate reasons not to intervene directly when doing so would create serious danger. Choosing another way to help, bringing in others, or finding someone better equipped to respond is not cowardice. The goal is not heroism. The goal is a constructive next step.

Someone should do something

Most of us have probably had the thought: Someone should do something. Sometimes someone already is. Sometimes the situation truly requires professionals, authorities, or people with specialized training.

But sometimes we are one of the people standing there.

Courage is the tool that helps us stay with the next question: Is there something I can do? The answer may be large or small. It may mean stepping forward, getting someone else involved, speaking when silence would be easier, or recognizing our own behavior and changing it.

At Peacewright Project, Courage is one tool in the craft of peacemaking. It does not ask us to be fearless. It asks us to keep fear from making every decision.
